# Frameworks behind the interview

Seven sources, condensed to what the interview actually uses. Read once before question 1. Paraphrase these ideas in conversation; don't lecture the founder about them.

## 1. PostHog: ICP vs persona, and validating with data

Source: PostHog newsletter, "The ideal customer profile framework".

- An ICP describes the companies (or people) who need the product and will pay: type, size, who their customers are, who uses it, what they replace, what they value. A persona describes one user's motivations. Building only for a persona gets you love without revenue.
- A narrow ICP shapes everything downstream: pricing, sales motion, content tone, roadmap.
- Validate with behaviour, not interviews alone: in-ICP customers should retain better, use more, and refer more.
- Real enthusiasm: inviting colleagues unprompted, heavy use of a rough product, referrals, wanting to pay. Polite interest: "interesting", "cool", won't pay, hands you to someone junior.
- Don't lock in on one or two outlier customers. Five similar paying customers is a good sign, ten is strong.

**Use it for:** pushing back on "they loved it" and asking what happened next.

## 2. Lenny Rachitsky: three narrow attributes and the signs you've found it

Source: Lenny's Newsletter, "How to identify your ideal customer profile (ICP)".

- Most founders got the ICP wrong at first. Successful ones picked at least three attributes and went uncomfortably narrow.
- Attribute categories: company size, job title, the pain, how the company works, tech they use, business type, price point, geography, where the user spends time.
- Outbound data is a cleaner signal than leads from investors and friends.
- Signs you're close: conversion jumps, enthusiasm jumps, urgency to act now, and "the nod" of instant recognition.
- Examples: Gusto stacked six filters (tiny California companies, no benefits, salaried only); Gong sold only to US software companies selling over video in English.

**Use it for:** turning categories into three or more concrete attributes.

## 3. Rahul Vohra / Superhuman: measure it and segment by who loves you

Source: First Round Review, "How Superhuman built an engine to find product-market fit".

- Ask active users how they'd feel if they could no longer use the product. 40% "very disappointed" is the benchmark; about 40 responses is enough to be directional.
- Segment by the "very disappointed" group. Their roles and their own descriptions of who benefits become the ICP and the copy.
- Ignore the "not disappointed". Work on what fans love and what holds back near-fans who already get the main benefit.

**Use it for:** products with users. Suggest running the survey when the founder has 40+ active users.

## 4. April Dunford: start from the alternatives

Source: Obviously Awesome; aprildunford.com quickstart guide.

- Order: competitive alternatives → differentiated capabilities → value → who cares most about that value (the best-fit customer) → market category.
- Ask what customers would do if you didn't exist. "Hire someone", "do it ourselves" and "do nothing" are real competitors.
- The same product can win with a different customer: look at the few who use it wildly well and ask why.

**Use it for:** finding what the founder actually replaces, and anchoring price against it.

## 5. Rob Fitzpatrick: The Mom Test

- Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about specific past behaviour, not hypothetical futures. Listen more than you talk.
- Compliments are noise. Commitments (time, money, an intro to the decision-maker) are signal.

**Use it for:** the founder's own customer conversations, and for judging the evidence they report to you.

## 6. Bob Moesta: the struggling moment

Source: Demand-Side Sales; Jobs to Be Done.

- Demand comes from a struggling moment, not a pitch. No struggle, no demand.
- Four forces: push of the current situation and pull of the new solution, against anxiety about the new and habit of the present.
- An ICP needs a trigger (when), not just firmographics (who).
- Weaker for impulse and consumer purchases, where the moment is fuzzier.

**Use it for:** the trigger question, and explaining lost "nice-to-have" deals.

## 7. Retool: hypotheses you kill, and language-market fit

Source: First Round Review, "Retool's path to product-market fit".

- Treat each ICP as a hypothesis and test it with cold outreach. Retool's first two guesses died fast; the third (developers at larger companies) worked.
- The words matter: the same product pitched in the customer's own language gets instant replies.
- Warm intros produce polite, vague meetings that fake fit.

**Use it for:** checking whether wins came only from warm relationships, and rewriting the pitch in customers' words.

## How they combine

1. Guess three narrow attributes (Lenny), anchored on the alternative (Dunford) and the trigger (Moesta).
2. Test with cold outreach in the customer's words (Retool), using honest questions (Mom Test).
3. Measure and segment by who loves it (Vohra); confirm in-ICP customers retain and spend more (PostHog).
4. Narrow until it feels uncomfortable; expand only after winning that group.
