# Writing-pattern evidence

Use this catalog to diagnose, not to run mechanical find-and-replace. The same construction can be useful in one passage and generic in another.

## Evidence tiers

### Substantive defect

Report separately from style:

- unsupported or unverifiable facts, comparisons, testimonials, or statistics;
- attribution that hides the source behind “experts,” “research,” or “people say”;
- a rewrite that removes meaningful uncertainty, legal scope, or technical precision;
- placeholders presented as finished content;
- product terminology that contradicts observed behavior.

Do not repair a missing fact by inventing one.

### High-confidence default

One occurrence may justify an edit when the construction contributes no information in context:

- throat-clearing that only announces a point;
- importance language that tells the reader how consequential a fact is instead of showing why;
- a question answered immediately for theatrical effect;
- a closing aphorism unrelated to the concrete conclusion;
- adjacent passages that make the same claim without new detail;
- generic transitions left from drafting scaffolds.

Even here, preserve a construction when it carries a documented voice function, real contrast, pacing, or necessary orientation.

### Strong presumption

Require contextual justification before keeping repeated patterns such as:

- manufactured binary reversals or clipped negative lists;
- padded triplets whose third item exists only for cadence;
- benefit language that obscures the capability or mechanism;
- inflated verbs and nominalizations where a plain verb is clearer;
- empty superlatives and adjectives without a measurable referent;
- synonym cycling that makes one concept look like several;
- audience flattery that avoids naming the real reader;
- decorative formatting that does not improve navigation or emphasis.

### Contextual signal

Flag only when repeated, mismatched to the format, or costly to comprehension:

- em dashes, fragments, rhetorical questions, contractions, passive voice;
- jargon, long sentences, short paragraphs, bullets, bold text, emoji;
- first or second person, humor, asides, metaphors, parentheticals;
- sentences beginning with conjunctions;
- repeated words used intentionally for clarity.

## Tests for a proposed edit

Ask:

1. Does deleting or changing this lose a claim, relationship, voice cue, or useful pace?
2. Is the problem the device itself, or its repetition and placement?
3. Does the replacement use information actually present in the draft?
4. Would the author's strong untouched sentences plausibly contain the replacement?
5. Does the format reward this construction—for example, repetition in audio or exact terminology in documentation?

If the evidence is weak, leave the line alone.
